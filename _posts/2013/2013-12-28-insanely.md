These days I learnt that there's no such thing as *too simple*. After years of design work, that's a relief. I've told clients that simple is the way to go, but they always asked for more beautifully complex things.

#### The books

It all started when I found out about *Insanely Simple*, a book by [Ken Segall](http://kensegall.com/). Even though I only read the first chapter, it changed everything for me. I got the idea — and the idea was *insanely simple*. In two days I rethought, rather than redesigned, both my website and this web service I'm actually writing on. It's simply amazing.

But there's always a *but*. Simplicity can be dangerous for text-based interfaces. You can't just splash some text on a site and think you've done something wonderful. That's why we have this thing called typography — and there's an insanely simple book for that too. Matthew Butterick's [Practical Typography](http://practicaltypography.com/) is the body, the head and the legs for anyone who wants to design typographically rich interfaces. Again, I got the idea by reading the [Typography in Ten Minutes](http://practicaltypography.com/typography-in-ten-minutes.html) introduction Butterick wrote. I took the whole night, skipped what I already knew, and finished the book.

#### The benefits

The benefits are enormous. I'm more focused on the things that actually matter — like the heading text rather than the style of the header. I've changed my mind about line height: it can't be higher than 3/2, and it should be slightly lower, to keep the reader's eyes on the text. Information density increased greatly for both Markdawn and my website. They're now more suitable for small-screen devices. That's a huge win — the best Christmas gift I could give myself, from myself.

If you find this text boring, there's a blog I recommend where you can enjoy [simple aesthetics](http://simply--aesthetic.tumblr.com/). Then maybe you'll want to come back, read the books and leave a remark.
